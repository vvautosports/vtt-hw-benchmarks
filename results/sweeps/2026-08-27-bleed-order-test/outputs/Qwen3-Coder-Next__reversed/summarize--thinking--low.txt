=== CONTENT ===
- Speculative decoding accelerates autoregressive inference by having a cheap draft mechanism propose multiple tokens, which the target model verifies in one batched forward pass; accepted tokens retain large-model quality at near draft-model cost, while rejections truncate the proposal and resume from the last accepted position, preserving distributional equivalence to standard decoding.  
- Draft mechanisms include separate small models, n-gram lookups, or multi-token-prediction (MTP) heads trained jointly with the target model to predict future tokens from the same hidden state.  
- Economic viability varies by workload: n-gram drafting excels for code/structured text with high repetition and acceptance rates, while open-ended prose yields lower acceptance and can be slower if the draft is poorly matched; MTP heads offer stable acceptance across domains at a modest memory/compute cost.  
- Mixture-of-experts (MoE) models reduce per-token active parameters but compromise speculation’s batching benefit because multiple positions may route to different experts, increasing memory bandwidth usage and raising the break-even acceptance rate compared to dense models.  
- Server-side parallelism complicates tuning: slot-based servers trade per-stream speed for aggregate throughput, and speculation competes with additional slots for compute headroom, requiring joint optimization of speculative mode, slot count, and sampling settings—evaluated via end-to-end tokens per second on representative tasks rather than microbenchmarks.

TL;DR: Speculative decoding trades draft accuracy and hardware constraints against batching gains and server throughput, with optimal configuration depending jointly on workload, model architecture (e.g., MoE vs. dense), and server setup.
