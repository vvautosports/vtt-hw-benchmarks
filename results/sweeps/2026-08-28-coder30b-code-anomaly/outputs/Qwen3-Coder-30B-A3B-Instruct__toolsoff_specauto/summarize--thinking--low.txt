=== CONTENT ===
- Speculative decoding speeds up autoregressive inference by having a cheap draft mechanism propose multiple tokens, which the target model then verifies in one batched forward pass; accepted tokens are kept at high-quality, while rejected ones trigger resumption from the last accepted position, maintaining distributional equivalence to standard decoding.  
- Draft mechanisms can be separate small models, n-gram lookups, or multi-token-prediction (MTP) heads trained jointly with the target model; MTP heads offer stable acceptance rates across domains because they see the same context as the target.  
- The economic benefit of speculation varies significantly by workload: n-gram drafting works well for repetitive text like code, while open-ended prose sees lower acceptance rates, and poor draft-model alignment can slow down generation compared to running the target alone.  
- Mixture-of-experts (MoE) models introduce complexity because speculative verification batches multiple positions that may route to different experts, increasing memory demands and reducing the batching advantage, raising the required acceptance rate for speculation to be profitable compared to dense models.  
- Server-side parallelism adds another layer of complexity, where slot-based systems must balance speculative decoding with concurrent sequences and compute headroom; thus, optimization requires treating speculative mode, slot count, and sampling settings together, evaluated via end-to-end throughput rather than microbenchmarks.

TL;DR: Speculative decoding improves autoregressive inference by having a draft model propose tokens verified in batch, but its efficiency depends heavily on the workload, model architecture (e.g., MoE vs. dense), and system-level factors like parallelism and memory constraints.
