Here is a summary of the passage:

- **Speculative decoding replaces slow token-by-token generation** by having an inexpensive draft mechanism (small model, n-gram lookup, or multi-token-prediction head) propose multiple tokens upfront, which the target model then verifies in a single batched forward pass; accepted tokens retain large-model quality at near-draft cost, and rejected proposals are simply truncated to resume from the last valid position.
- **Acceptance rates—and thus speedups—vary sharply by workload:** code and structured text benefit from long repeated spans that n-gram drafting captures efficiently, open-ended prose yields fewer accepted tokens (and risks slowdowns if the draft is poorly matched), while MTP heads offer stable acceptance across domains at the cost of modest extra compute and memory.
- **Mixture-of-experts models face a unique bandwidth bottleneck:** speculative verification can route different positions to different experts, forcing more expert weights through memory per step and eroding the batching advantage that makes speculation profitable on bandwidth-bound hardware, meaning MoE targets require higher acceptance rates to break even compared to dense models.
- **On servers, speculative decoding competes with parallel slot allocation for the same compute headroom,** so increasing slot count (which raises aggregate throughput but lowers per-stream speed) and running in speculative mode are opposing forces rather than independent settings.
- **Optimal configuration treats speculative mode, slot count, and sampling as a joint problem** measured by end-to-end tokens per second on representative tasks, rather than tuning each knob independently or relying on microbenchmarks.

**TL;DR:** Speculative decoding can significantly speed up autoregressive inference, but its benefits depend heavily on workload type, model architecture (especially MoE), and server configuration, requiring holistic tuning measured in real-world end-to-end throughput.
