- **How it works:** Speculative decoding speeds up autoregressive inference by having a cheap draft mechanism propose several tokens that the target model verifies in a single batched forward pass; accepted tokens arrive at large-model quality near draft-model cost, a rejection truncates the proposal and resumes from the last accepted position, so outputs stay distributionally identical to standard decoding.

- **Draft options:** The draft can be a separate small model, an n-gram lookup over the prompt and generation history, or a newer multi-token-prediction (MTP) head trained jointly with the target that predicts a short window of future tokens from the same hidden state.

- **Workload economics:** Code and structured text repeat long spans, giving high n-gram acceptance almost for free; open-ended prose accepts fewer tokens, and a poorly matched draft can even slow generation by wasting full verification passes; MTP heads sit in between, with acceptance stable across domains because the head sees the exact context the target sees.

- **MoE complication:** For mixture-of-experts targets the break-even acceptance rate is higher than for dense ones, because batched verification may route each position to different experts, pulling more expert weights through memory per step and eroding the batching advantage that makes speculation profitable—especially on bandwidth-bound hardware.

- **Server tuning:** Slot-based servers split the KV-cache budget across sequences (more slots raise throughput but lower per-stream speed), and speculation competes with extra slots for the same compute headroom, so speculative mode, slot count, and sampling settings form one joint configuration to be evaluated on end-to-end tokens per second rather than trusted microbenchmarks.

**TL;DR:** Speculative decoding lowers inference cost, but its actual speedup depends on matching the draft to the workload and co-tuning it with MoE routing and server slot/sampling settings as a single joint configuration rather than independent knobs.
